\section{The Story of Characters, Told in Broad Strokes} \label{sec:broad-strokes} % SECTION:
\pan\textit{If you want, this section charts the ``etymology'' of each word in the dictionary!}
\par The slogan is that \textbf{characters decategorify modules}, in analogy to how \textbf{dimension decategorifies vector spaces}.
\begin{align*}
  \begin{tikzcd}[ampersand replacement = \;]
    % Title row
      \text{Categorified}
        \arrow[rr, "\text{character}"]
      \;
      \; \text{Decategorified}
      \; \text{What is lost?}
    \\
    % Top row
      \text{Module } U
        \arrow[d, "\text{morphism space}" description]
        \arrow[rr, mapsto]
      \;
      \; \text{List of numbers } \chi_U \in \mathbb{C}^G
        \arrow[d, "\text{its dimension}" description]
      \; \text{Algebraic data (action } {G \to \GL(U)})
        \arrow[d, "\text{transformation of maps}" description]
    \\
      \text{Module } V
        \arrow[rr, mapsto]
      \;
      \; \text{List of numbers } \chi_V \in \mathbb{C}^G
      \; \text{Algebraic data: (action } {G \to \GL(V)})
  \end{tikzcd}
\end{align*}
\pan To exemplify the analogy:
\begin{align*}
  \begin{tikzcd}[ampersand replacement = \;]
    % Title row
      \text{Categorified}
        \arrow[rr, "\text{dimension}"]
      \;
      \; \text{Decategorified}
      \; \text{What is lost?}
    \\
    % Top row
      \text{Vect. Sp. } U
        \arrow[d, "\text{morphism space}" description]
        \arrow[rr, mapsto]
      \;
      \; \text{Number } \dim U \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
        \arrow[d, "\text{rank-nullity statements}" description]
      \; \text{Algebraic data (vectors)}
        \arrow[d, "\text{transformation of algebraic data}" description]
    \\
      \text{Vect. Sp. } V
        \arrow[rr, mapsto]
      \;
      \; \text{Number } \dim V \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
      \; \text{Algebraic data (vectors)}
  \end{tikzcd}
\end{align*}
\par In both situations, \textbf{the decategorification classifies the categorification up to isomorphism} -- that is, two (finite-dimensional) vector spaces are isomorphic just when they have the same dimension, and two (finite-dimensional, complex) $G$-modules are isomorphic just when they have the same character. At the level of objects, the decategorification retains all necessary information up to isomorphism \ignore{(as one would hope of anything being granted the monacre of ``decategorification'')}. However, passing to this ``more computational invariant'' has the deficiency of losing algebraic data. On decategorifying $\FinVect$, we lose information on how linear maps put together vector spaces. On decategorifying $\FinVect^G$, we lose understanding of the actual actions of $G$ and how these actions intertwine.

\skoop
\subsection{Prologue: The Birth of Characters} % SECTION:
\par We now tell a fiction on the story of character theory, all in broad strokes. Once upon a time, vector spaces were decategorified by their dimension, and everyone lived happily ever after. Well, they lived up until algebraists decided to represent symmetries on vector spaces, moving us from the land $\FinVect$ to the various lands $\FinVect^G$ (for $G : \FinGroup$). In early and tumultuous days, many discoveries were made paralleling the story for vector spaces:
\begin{align*}
  \begin{tabular}{l|cc}
    & Vector spaces & Modules \\
    \hline
    can be broken up into
      & one-dimensional subspaces,
      & irreducible submodules;
    \\
    the maps between which are
      & effectively scalars,
      & effectively scalars;
    \\
    and which form all objects by
      & direct sum,
      & direct sum.
  \end{tabular}
\end{align*}
\par After these initial algebraic gains, however, lingered important questions -- ``How many irreducible modules are there?'', or ``Where can we find these modules?'', for example. To gain further ground, the category $\FinVect^G$ needed to be quite seriously tamed. After dusting off the history books, the algebraists attempted to decategorify $\FinVect^G$ by mirroring the dimension decategorification of $\FinVect$. Fortunately, in the context of a group representation, the dimension appears naturally:
\begin{align*}
  G \xrightarrow{\rho_V} \GL(V)
  \quad\rightsquigarrow\quad
  \rho_V (1_G)
  = 1_V
  = \underbrace{
    \begin{pmatrix}
      1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
      0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\
      \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
      0 & 0 & \cdots & 1
    \end{pmatrix}
  }_{n \times n}
  \quad\rightsquigarrow\quad
  \dim(V)
  = n
  = \tr (\rho_V (1_G))
  .
\end{align*}
\par This is a natural choice because the representing linear transformations $\rho_V (g), g \in G$ are the only extra data present within objects of $\FinVect^G$, and the only such transformation guaranteed to exist is $\rho_V (1_G) = 1_V$. The generalisation easily revealed itself -- the \textbf{character} of $V$ is the function $\chi_V : G \to \mathbb{C}$ given by $\chi_V := \tr \circ \rho_V$. The miracle is that this successfully decategorified away enough complexity to make tractable progress in understanding the category $\FinVect^G$.

\subsection{Chapter One: Separating Modules with Inner Products} % SECTION:
\par The preceding text is best understood as a prologue to our story -- ``Chapter $0$'', if you will. In chapter one, we begin to understand the hom-spaces $\FinVect^G (U, V)$ for $U, V : \FinVect^G$. Every part of our story takes place over the category of vector spaces, so it is not a surprise to find that $\FinVect^G (U, V)$ is a vector space. Recording its dimension would be a first feat, and no easy one at that. In perhaps a stroke of luck, or perhaps by arcane insight dug up from historical texts\footnote{The \textit{Module-Wrestler Online Character Dictionary} keeps no good record of history.}, one can find some inspiration in a superficial\footnote{Part of the superficiality is not adequately keeping track of the opposite category.} look at $\FinVect^G(\blank, \blank)$:
\begin{align*}
  \begin{tikzcd}[ampersand replacement = \;, row sep = tiny]
    \text{Categorified} \arrow[dddd, "\text{character}"']
      \; \FinVect^G \times \FinVect^G
        \arrow[r]
      \; \FinVect
    \\
    %
      \; (U, V)
        \arrow[r, mapsto]
      \; \FinVect^G (U, V)
      ,
    \\
    % blank row
    \\
    % blank row
    \\
    \text{Decategorified}
      \; \mathbb{C}^G \times \mathbb{C}^G
        \arrow[r, dashed]
      \; \mathbb{C}
    \\
    %
      \; (\chi_U, \chi_V)
        \arrow[r, mapsto]
      \; {?}
      .
  \end{tikzcd}
\end{align*}
\par The superficial symmetry is that $\FinVect^G (\blank, \blank)$ appears to decategorify into something resembling an inner product. There is a natural choice of inner product on the set of functions $G \to \mathbb{C}$, given by
\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{C}^G \times \mathbb{C}^G
  &\xrightarrow{\langle \blank, \blank \rangle}
  \mathbb{C}
  \\
  (\phi, \psi)
  &\longmapsto
  \frac{1}{\lvert G \rvert} \sum_{g \in G} \phi(g) \cdot \overline{\psi(g)}
  .
\end{align*}
\par The division by $\lvert G \rvert$ may be seen as a normalisation factor; this had proven useful to the algebraists prior. The miracle of chapter one is that \textit{this inner product idea worked} and gave rise to a rich theory, eventually giving the dimension formula
\begin{align*}
  \dim \FinVect^G (U, V)
  &= \langle \chi_U, \chi_V \rangle
\end{align*}
and answering some of the lingering questions prior. How these results follow is not immediate, and will take us another chapter to describe.

\subsection{Chapter Two: Orthonormality of Irreducible Characters} % SECTION:
\par A first observation is that
\begin{lemma}[Characters of direct sums decompose as sums of characters]
\label{result:character-direct-sum}
  \pan Let $\set*{ V_{\alpha} }_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}}$ be objects of $\FinVect^G$. Then, $\chi_{\oplus_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}} V_{\alpha}} = \sum_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}} \chi_{V_{\alpha}}$.
\end{lemma}
\par Since any module decomposes uniquely into a direct sum of irreducible submodules, we should first study the characters $\chi_{I}$ where $I$ is irreducible. If our inner product $\langle \blank, \blank \rangle$ really is to count dimensions of hom-spaces, then Schur's lemma tells us we should expect that
\begin{align*}
  U \simeq V \text{ are irreducible}
  \implies
  \FinVect^G (U, V) \simeq_{\FinVect} \eqmathbox[schur-result][l]{\mathbb{C}^1}
  \implies
  \langle \chi_U, \chi_V \rangle
  = 1
  ,
  \\
  U \not\simeq V \text{ are irreducible}
  \implies
  \FinVect^G (U, V) \simeq_{\FinVect} \eqmathbox[schur-result][c]{0}
  \implies
  \langle \chi_U, \chi_V \rangle
  = 0
  ,
\end{align*}
which is to say in other words that if we enumerate the characters as $\chi_I$, one for each isomorphism class of irreducible modules $I : \FinVect^G$, then these characters are orthonormal. Sure enough,
\begin{proposition}[Orthonormality of irreducible characters]
\label{result:orthogonality}
  \pan For irreducible modules $I, J : \FinVect^G$,
  \begin{align*}
    \langle \chi_I, \chi_J \rangle
    &= \one_{I \simeq J}
    ,
  \end{align*}
  with the $\one$ denoting an indicator function. \ignore{(Perhaps think of the Kronecker delta.)}
\end{proposition}
\par Now, let us collect together one irreducible $G$-module from each isomorphism class into a set $\Irred(G)$ \ignore{(ignore size issues; they can be fixed)} and write two direct sum decompositions $U \simeq \bigoplus_{I \in \Irred(G)} I^{\oplus u_I}$ and $V \simeq \bigoplus_{I \in \Irred(G)} I^{\oplus v_I}$. Considering the universal properties of the direct sum, Schur's lemma tells us that
\begin{align*}
  \FinVect^G (U, V)
  &\simeq \bigoplus_{I, J \in \Irred(G)} \FinVect^G (I, J)^{\oplus u_I v_J}
  \\
  &\simeq \bigoplus_{I, J \in \Irred(G)} \left( \mathbb{C}^{\one_{I = J}} \right)^{\oplus u_I v_J}
  \\
  \FinVect^G (U, V)
  &\simeq \bigoplus_{I \in \Irred(G)} \mathbb{C}^{\oplus u_I v_I}
  \\
  \implies\quad
  \dim \FinVect^G (U, V)
  &= \sum_{I \in \Irred(G)} u_I v_I
\end{align*}
while if we compute the characters, the previous proposition shows us that
\begin{align*}
  \langle \chi_U, \chi_V \rangle
  &= \sum_{I, J \in \Irred(G)} u_I v_J \langle \chi_I, \chi_J \rangle
  \\
  &= \sum_{I, J \in \Irred(G)} u_I v_J \one_{I = J}
  \\
  \langle \chi_U, \chi_V \rangle
  &= \sum_{I \in \Irred(G)} u_I v_I
\end{align*}
and therefore,
\begin{theorem}[Inner products of characters decategorify hom-spaces]
\label{result:inner-product-dimension}
  \pan For $U, V : \FinVect^G$, we have
  \begin{align*}
    \langle \chi_U, \chi_V \rangle
    &= \dim \FinVect^G (U, V)
    .
  \end{align*}
\end{theorem}
\par What we have seen also gives us a strategy to extract from a character $\chi_V = \sum_{i \in \Irred(G)} a_i \cdot \chi_{V_i}$ the coefficients $a_i \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ declaring the ``multiplicities'' in the direct sum $V \simeq \bigoplus_{I \in \Irred(G)} I^{\oplus a_I}$ of irreducible modules. One can either leverage orthonormality of the irreducible characters (to extract the coefficients straight away), or leverage the fact that $a_i = \dim \FinVect^G (I, V)$ \ignore{(which is not hard to prove)}. Either way,
\begin{theorem}[Irreducible characters give an orthonormal basis for all characters]
\label{result:irreducible-basis}
  \pan Just as the irreducible submodules give a ``direct sum basis'' for the finite-dimensional complex $G$-modules up to isomorphism, the irreducible characters give a ``$\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$-weighted basis'' for their characters. That is, we have a correspondence
  \begin{align*}
    \FinVect^G_{/{\simeq}}
    &\longleftrightarrow
    \set*{ \text{characters of } G }
    \\
    V
    \simeq \bigoplus_{I \in \Irred(G)} I^{\oplus a_I}
    &\longleftrightarrow
    \chi_V
    = \sum_{I \in \Irred(G)} a_I \cdot \chi_{I}
    .
  \end{align*}
  % where $\Irred(G)$ collects each isomorphism class of irreducible $G$-modules. \ignore{(Ignore, for now, the worry that constructing $\Irred(G)$ may seem to ask for a choice function over proper classes. We will later provide a recipe in the language of group representations ot construct $\Irred(G)$.)}
\end{theorem}
\pan Read the right way, the previous result entails that
\begin{theorem}[Equality of characters decategorifies equivalence of modules]
\label{result:character-equality-decategorifies}
  \pan For $U, V : \FinVect^G$, we have
  \begin{align*}
    U \simeq V
    &\iff \chi_U = \chi_V
    .
  \end{align*}
\end{theorem}
\par Our result \ref{result:irreducible-characters-orthonormal-basis} on a ``basis'' for the set of characters has a linear algebraic deficiency. It would be nice to interpret this as an actual basis for a vector space, but the set of characters is not closed under scaling by $\mathbb{C}$. Rather than doing something artificial (e.g. taking the $\mathbb{C}$-span of this set), we can consider the vector space $\mathbb{C}^G$. Thinking categorically, we find that
\begin{lemma}[Characters are class functions]
\label{result:characters-class-functions}
  \pan Just as conjugate \ignore{(isomorphic)} elements $x$ and $g x g^{-1}$ of a group have similar \ignore{(isomorphic)} representing matrices $\rho_V (x)$ and $\rho_V (g) \rho_V (x) \rho_V (g)^{-1}$, the character assigns these elements equal values. That is, $\chi_V (x) = \chi_V (g x g^{-1})$. \ignore{(This decategorifies isomorphism into equality.)}
\end{lemma}
\par This does mean that characters cannot span $\mathbb{C}^G$ for non-Abelian $G$. However, it also shows us a better target for their span.
\begin{theorem}[Irreducible characters give an orthonormal basis for class functions]
\label{result:irreducible-characters-orthonormal-basis}
  \pan The set $\set*{ \chi_I \given I \in \Irred(G) }$ is an orthonormal basis for $\mathbb{C}^{\Classes(G)}$. There is hence one irreducible character of $G$ for each conjugacy class of $G$. Categorifying, there is \ignore{(up to isomorphism)} one irreducible module of $G$ for each conjugacy class of $G$.
\end{theorem}
\pan We can now conclude that $\Irred(G)$ is a finite set.

\subsection{Chapter Three: Bonus Goodies} % SECTION:
\par All good things come in threes, and the missing partner to characters and conjugacy is the left-regular representation $\mathbb{C}[G]$. A telling of this story which does not take such broad strokes would actually reveal, in showing that the irreducible characters span $\mathbb{C}^{\Classes(G)}$ (as part of proving \ref{result:irreducible-characters-orthonormal-basis}), that the left-regular representation appears. To take yet another broad stroke, the conclusion here is that this representation can be completely torn apart.
\begin{proposition}[The left-regular representation summarises the irreducible modules]
\label{result:kill-left-regular}
  \pan The left-regular representation $\mathbb{C}[G]$ decomposes as
  \begin{align*}
    \mathbb{C}[G]
    &\simeq \bigoplus_{I \in \Irred(G)} I^{\oplus \dim I}
    ,
  \end{align*}
  or in the language of characters,
  \begin{align*}
    \chi_{\mathbb{C}[G]}
    &= \sum_{I \in \Irred(G)} \dim I \cdot \chi_I
    .
  \end{align*}
  \pan Furthermore, the centre of the endomorphism algebra captures the conjugacy classes and irreducible modules together:
  \begin{align*}
    \lvert \Classes(G) \rvert
    = \dim\left(
      Z\left(
        \FinVect^G (\mathbb{C}[G], \mathbb{C}[G])
      \right)
    \right)
    = \lvert \Irred(G) \rvert
    .
  \end{align*}
  \pan This gives another proof, independent of \ref{result:irreducible-characters-orthonormal-basis}, that irreducible characters biject with conjugacy classes.
\end{proposition}
\par If your goal is to apply representation theory to distinguish groups, the story told thus far should guide you towards finding characters. The ``holy grail'' in this avenue is to list the character table of any finite groups you care about (see section \ref{sec:tables}), and point at different tables to tell apart many groups. However, before you go out on such an odyssey, let us close this story with some words on induction and restriction.
\begin{theorem}[Frobenius reciprocity]
\label{result:frobenius-reciprocity}
  \pan Fix $H \leq G$ in $\FinGroup$ and an enumeration $\set*{x_{\alpha} H}_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}}$ of the cosets in $G / H$. The adjoint functors $\Ind_H^G \dashv \Res^G_H$ act in characters by
  \begin{align*}
    \begin{tikzcd}[ampersand replacement = \;]
      \sum_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}} \hat{\chi}_U (x_{\alpha}^{-1} (\blank) x_{\alpha})
      \; \set*{\text{char's of } G}
      \; \FinVect^G
        \arrow[d, shift left, "\Res^G_H"]
        \arrow[r, "\text{char.}"]
        \arrow[l, "\text{char.}"']
      \; \set*{\text{char's of } G}
        \arrow[d, dashed]
      \; \chi_V
        \arrow[d, mapsto]
      \\
      \chi_U
        \arrow[u, mapsto]
      \; \set*{\text{char's of } H}
        \arrow[u, dashed]
      \; \FinVect^H
        \arrow[u, shift left, "\Ind_H^G"]
        \arrow[r, "\text{char.}"']
        \arrow[l, "\text{char.}"]
      \; \set*{\text{char's of } H}
      \; \restrict{\chi_V}_{H}
    \end{tikzcd}
  \end{align*}
  where $\hat{\chi}_U : G \to \mathbb{C}$ is given by $\hat{\chi}_U (h) = \chi_U (h)$ for $h \in H$ and $\hat{\chi}_U (g) = 0$ for $g \in G \setminus H$. This gives a formula to define induction and restriction operators on the space of class functions,
  \begin{align*}
    \begin{tikzcd}[ampersand replacement = \;]
      \sum_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}} \hat{\varphi} (x_{\alpha}^{-1} (\blank) x_{\alpha})
      \; \mathbb{C}^{\Classes(G)}
        \arrow[d, shift left, dashed, "\res^G_H"]
      \; \psi
        \arrow[d, mapsto]
      \\
      \varphi
        \arrow[u, mapsto]
      \; \mathbb{C}^{\Classes(H)}
        \arrow[u, shift left, dashed, "\ind_H^G"]
      \; \restrict{\psi}_{H}
      ,
    \end{tikzcd}
  \end{align*}
  and the formula for $\ind^G_H$ is independent of the representatives $x_{\alpha}$. The fact that the functors are adjoint appears in characters as the linear operators being Hermitian adjoints.
  \begin{align*}
    \FinVect^G \left(
      \Ind^G_H U
    , V
    \right)
    \simeq \FinVect^H \left(
      U
    , \Res^G_H V
    \right)
    \quad\rightsquigarrow\quad
    \langle \ind_H^G \chi_U, \chi_V \rangle_{G}
    = \langle \chi_U, \res^G_H \chi_V \rangle_{H}
  \end{align*}
\end{theorem}
